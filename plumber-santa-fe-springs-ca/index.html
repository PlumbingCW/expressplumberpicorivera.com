When a drain or supply line stops a Santa Fe Springs workday

Santa Fe Springs has industrial and commercial properties mixed in with its homes, so a plumbing failure here often means a business is shut down until it is fixed. A floor drain that overflows in a back room, a restroom line that will not clear, or a burst supply pipe over inventory can all turn into an emergency within the hour.

For a building, the first decision is whether to stop water at the main, at a branch valve or at the fixture. For a home, it is usually the main. Either way, the faster the water is stopped, the less drying and replacement follows, and the 24/7 line at (562) 488-9614 is there for exactly that moment.

Before we arrive: an active leak at a shop, warehouse or house

These four steps hold the damage down until the crew gets there.

Close the right valve

Find the main shutoff for the unit and turn it clockwise. In a shared commercial building, the landlord or property manager may hold the master valve, so call them as well.

Cut power near wet equipment

Switch off the breaker for any panel, machine or appliance the water is touching. Never stand in water to do it.

Move stock and records

Lift boxes, paper files and electronics off the floor and away from the leak path. Photograph the damage for your own records.

Ring the 24/7 line

Tell dispatch whether the building is a home or a commercial unit and whether water is still flowing. Reach us on the emergency number at any hour.

What 69.2 percent pre-1980 housing means for the lines

The Census ACS 2024 puts the share of Santa Fe Springs housing built before 1980 at 69.2 percent. That is a majority, but a smaller one than in some neighboring cities, so a home on your street may be newer than you assume.

Check the year built on your own house before deciding on large work. A property from the 1950s or 1960s is a stronger candidate for line replacement than one from the late 1970s, and a camera inspection tells you which case you are in.

Where the tap water comes from

The City of Santa Fe Springs 2024 Water Quality Report describes a mix of treated groundwater from deep wells in the Whittier Narrows area, treated groundwater from the Central Basin facility, and filtered surface water from the Metropolitan Water District. The report is the place to look for current numbers.

We did not read a hardness figure, so we are not quoting one. Pull up the report or test your tap before you decide on a softener or a heater with a long warranty, and mention the result when you call (562) 488-9614.
