Hard Water Scale in Pico Rivera, CA: What 174.4 mg/L Means for Your Plumbing

By Pico Rivera Express Plumbing · Updated October 23, 2026 · 6 min read

Average hardness in the City of Pico Rivera's 2023 water quality report is 174.4 mg/L, equal to roughly 10.2 grains per gallon. That is on the hard side and is why scale builds up on fixtures and in water heaters.

The number, in plain terms

One grain per gallon equals 17.1 mg/L, so 174.4 mg/L divides out to about 10.2 grains per gallon. On the scale Cal Water prints in its report, hard water runs 150 to 300 ppm, which puts Pico Rivera's average in that band.

Hard water is not a plumbing failure. It is minerals that leave a crust where water is heated or evaporates. If you see white crust, slow flow or a noisy heater, our team at (562) 488-9614 can look at what scale has done.

The number also helps you compare. If a neighbor in another town reports very different hardness, that is normal, since each water system draws from its own sources. Use your own utility's report as the baseline.

Local note

Pico Rivera water and housing: what it means for you

Local figures come from the City of Pico Rivera Water 2023 Consumer Confidence Report. The report traces the supply to deep wells in the local groundwater, with hardness averaging 174.4 mg/L and ranging from 121 to 212 mg/L. The city also lists an average sodium level of 59.6 mg/L.

Because the range is wide, your own water can be softer or harder than the average. A cheap test strip from a hardware store will show your tap. The city posts each year's report at pico-rivera.org/water-quality, so read your utility's annual water quality report before you buy equipment.

What scale does around the house

Mineral buildup shows up first where water is heated or splashed. Cal Water's report warns of mineral buildup in pipes and in water heaters.

Water heaters

Scale settles on heating surfaces and the tank floor. A heater may run louder or take longer to recover. Our Water Heater Repair team can flush and inspect a tank.

Faucets and showerheads

White crust clogs aerator screens and spray holes, so pressure drops at one fixture while others are fine.

Glass, tile and fixtures

Spots on shower doors and cloudy fixtures come from evaporated minerals. Wiping the glass after use and rinsing chrome slows the crust, but it will keep returning wherever water dries.

Pipes

Minerals can build up inside pipes. If several fixtures lose flow and aerators are clean, a Repiping review is worth asking about.

Test it, clean it, then decide

This sequence costs little and gives you real data before you buy a system.

  1. 1

    Test the tap

    Use a hardness test strip on a cold kitchen tap and note the color match. Compare the result with the Cal Water scale above, and test again at a bathroom tap in case the readings differ.

  2. 2

    Clean the easy spots

    Soak aerators and showerheads in white vinegar, then rinse. Run a flush of the water heater if it has not been done in a while.

  3. 3

    Look at the heater

    Ask a technician to check the tank for sediment and the relief valve for crust. If the tank is failing, Water Heater Replacement is the next conversation.

  4. 4

    Decide on treatment

    If scale keeps returning, look at a softener or other filtration. Read the salt caveats below first.

Softener basics and the salt caveats

A salt-based softener swaps hardness minerals for sodium. It protects heaters and fixtures but adds sodium to the water. Cal Water's report flags two issues. The salt some softeners add can cause trouble at wastewater treatment plants, and the added sodium matters to anyone on a low-sodium diet.

Check with your household and your utility before installing one. Ask whether a salt-free or filter-only option fits your goal, and what it will and will not change about scale. Our Water Softener and Filtration page covers the choices, and installers must size a unit to your flow and hardness.

A note for neighboring areas

Hardness differs from place to place. Golden State Water's Norwalk system report shows an average of 310 mg/L, far above Pico Rivera's number, so advice for Norwalk homes is not the same. In Commerce, Cal Water's report lists groundwater hardness averaging 146 ppm.
