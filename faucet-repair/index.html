Burst supply line or a valve that will not close: what to do first

Look under the sink for the two small shutoffs, one for hot and one for cold. Turn each clockwise until it stops. If the spray continues, or the shutoff itself is the thing leaking, go to the main water valve for the house and close that.

Open the faucet to drain the line, then put a bucket and towels under the leak. Keep power cords, a garbage disposal switch and any plug-in appliance away from the wet cabinet. After that, call the 24/7 line and tell us which valve would not hold, because that decides what we bring.

Tub and shower valves are harder, since many have no local shutoff. If the handle will not stop the flow, close the main valve and call at any hour. Dispatch answers these at any hour, and a same-day slot is offered when one is open.

Fixture by fixture: what goes wrong and what we do

Each fixture fails in its own way. Here is how we handle the four you are most likely to call about.

Kitchen faucets

Drips from the spout usually mean a worn cartridge or seat. A spray head that leaks, a loose base, or water under the sink points to the supply lines or the connections. If your sink also drains slowly, see Drain Cleaning.

Bathroom sink faucets

Single-handle models usually need a cartridge. Two-handle models usually need washers, seats or stems. Low flow from one side often points to a clogged aerator or a partly closed shutoff, which are quick checks.

Bathtub faucets and diverters

A tub spout that drips after the shower starts has a bad diverter. A handle that keeps the water running means the valve behind the wall has worn. We explain how we will reach it before opening anything.

Shower valves and showerheads

A showerhead that drips from the arm is often a thread seal. A valve that stays warm or drips from the head needs a cartridge. If you see stains on the ceiling below a shower, stop using it and ask us about the plumbing behind the wall.

A slow drip is not as small as it looks

The EPA says a faucet dripping once per second can waste more than 3,000 gallons a year. A showerhead leaking 10 drips a minute adds up to more than 500 gallons.

For you that means a repair is a decision about your bill as well as the noise. If the meter moves after you shut every tap, the leak is somewhere else, and our Leak Detection page covers how we find it. The EPA suggests checking the meter before and after a two-hour period when no water is used.

Replacing a failed supply stop: how the visit runs

Old shutoff valves are a common reason a simple faucet job turns into a bigger one.

  1. 1

    Close the main

    We shut the house water and open the lowest tap to drain the line.

  2. 2

    Remove the failed stop

    The old valve comes off the pipe and the supply line comes off the fixture. We check the pipe end for damage before fitting anything new.

  3. 3

    Fit and test

    A new stop and supply line go on, and we turn the water back on slowly while watching every joint.

  4. 4

    Confirm the fixture

    We run hot and cold and look under the sink for weeping after a short wait.

Tub and shower drains that will not clear

Bathtub and shower drains clog with hair and soap far more often than they fail. If water stands around your feet in the shower, that is a drain job, not a faucet job.

If a second fixture also pools, that points to the branch line. Our Hydro Jetting page covers the heavier cleaning we use when cable alone will not do it.
